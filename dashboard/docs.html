AI Satellite Collision Prevention System

Physics-based simulation and optimization for preventing orbital collisions among thousands of spacecraft — and minimizing damage when collisions are unavoidable.

🚨 The Problem: Low Earth Orbit Has No Traffic Laws

~10,000 active satellites circle Earth at roughly 28,000 km/h. No lanes, no traffic lights, no brakes — and when two of them meet, they don't just crash. They shatter, and the fragments become the next threat.

⚡
Kessler Syndrome — The Cascade Nobody Can Undo

A single collision between two satellites produces thousands of new fragments, each traveling fast enough to destroy something else. One collision creates more threats. Those threats create more collisions. Left unmanaged, certain orbital shells become permanently unusable — a scenario first proposed by NASA scientist Donald Kessler in 1978.

Avoiding this isn't as simple as "something is coming, move." Every spacecraft has to answer four questions before it burns fuel:

QuestionWhy it's hard
How much should I move?Too little wastes the maneuver; too much wastes irreplaceable fuel
When should I move?Earlier burns are cheaper, but the risk assessment is less certain
Will this maneuver create another collision later?Dodging one object can put you in the path of a different one
Is the fuel worth spending?Fuel spent today isn't available for a bigger threat next week

Today, these decisions can require hours of human analysis per conjunction. That doesn't scale past a few thousand satellites — let alone the 100,000+ objects the industry is heading toward.

The Four Constraints Every Decision Faces

⏳
1. Time

Conjunctions may be hours to days out, but human decision loops take hours themselves. At 14.2 km/s relative velocity, a 47-meter miss distance corresponds to roughly 3 milliseconds of timing error. There's no time for a meeting.

⛽
2. Fuel

Every maneuver consumes a finite, lifetime resource — typically 20-60 m/s of Δv for the entire mission. A bad decision today can shorten a satellite's operational life by years. There's no refueling mid-mission.

🔗
3. Coupling

Moving satellite A away from satellite B might put it closer to satellite C tomorrow. Conjunctions can't be optimized one at a time — the entire orbital environment has to be considered simultaneously.

📡
4. Connectivity

A satellite isn't always in view of a ground station. If a threat appears mid-pass over the Pacific, it can wait up to ~90 minutes — a full orbital period — before it can even start a conversation with its operator. It needs reflexes of its own.

🛰
Our Solution: Plan Around Collisions, Not Just React to Them

Orbital does in seconds what currently takes hours. The physics engine propagates every trajectory forward using J2 perturbations, atmospheric drag, and solar radiation pressure — and propagates the uncertainty in that trajectory too, because knowing where a satellite should be isn't enough; we need to know how wrong we might be.

Millions of candidate pairs are screened with fast geometric filters, and high-fidelity collision-risk math runs only on the handful of pairs that matter (see Conjunction Assessment below).

The key difference: we don't optimize one collision at a time — we optimize the entire threat network. Three strategies are compared for every decision:

  • • Greedy optimization — resolve the highest-risk conjunction first, for immediate threats
  • • Network flow optimization — fuel-optimal allocation across the whole constellation
  • • Monte Carlo Tree Search — 7-day lookahead across sequential maneuver decisions

So instead of asking "how do I avoid this collision," the system asks: "what decision leaves me safest over the next seven days?"

💥
And Sometimes You Can't Dodge

Sometimes the warning comes too late. Sometimes there's no fuel left. Sometimes the other object is uncontrollable debris. When avoidance isn't possible, the system switches from avoidance to damage mitigation — evaluating outcomes and selecting the scenario that minimizes downstream debris risk (see Damage Minimization below).

Even when physics wins, the damage can still be minimized.

The Core Optimization Problem

Given N spacecraft with uncertain trajectories and limited fuel, minimize long-term orbital risk:

$$J = \sum_j w_j \cdot P_{c,j}(\Delta\mathbf{v}_1 \ldots \Delta\mathbf{v}_N) + \lambda \sum_i \frac{|\Delta\mathbf{v}_i|}{\Delta v_{\text{budget},i}} + \gamma \cdot \text{Debris\_risk}$$

Where the weights balance collision probability reduction, fuel conservation, and long-term cascade risk (Kessler syndrome).

🌐 Orbital Mechanics

The two-body problem, perturbation forces, numerical integration, and State Transition Matrix propagation for uncertainty evolution.

Two-Body Equation of Motion
$$\ddot{\mathbf{r}} = -\frac{\mu}{|\mathbf{r}|^3}\mathbf{r}$$
Produces Keplerian conic section orbits
Orbital Period
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}$$
~92 min for LEO at 400 km altitude
Circular Velocity
$$v_c = \sqrt{\frac{\mu}{r}}$$
~7.67 km/s at 400 km altitude
Kepler's Equation
$$M = E - e\sin E$$
Solved iteratively via Newton-Raphson
🌎
J2 Oblateness Perturbation

Earth's equatorial bulge creates the dominant LEO perturbation:

Acceleration components $$a_x = -\frac{3}{2}\frac{J_2 \mu R_E^2}{r^5} x\left(1 - 5\frac{z^2}{r^2}\right)$$ $$a_z = -\frac{3}{2}\frac{J_2 \mu R_E^2}{r^5} z\left(3 - 5\frac{z^2}{r^2}\right)$$

Causes orbital plane precession at ~7°/day for typical LEO orbits. J₂ = 1.08263 × 10⁻³

🌀
Atmospheric Drag

Dominates below 1000 km. Exponential density model with scale heights:

Drag acceleration $$\mathbf{a}_{\text{drag}} = -\frac{1}{2}\rho C_d \frac{A}{m}|\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}|\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}$$

Key parameter: area-to-mass ratio (A/m). High A/m objects (solar panels, debris) decay faster.

Density model $$\rho(h) = \rho_0 \exp\left(-\frac{h - h_0}{H}\right)$$
📈
State Transition Matrix (STM)

Linearized sensitivity of state to initial conditions. Critical for covariance and maneuver planning:

STM evolution $$\dot{\Phi} = F \cdot \Phi, \quad \Phi(t_0, t_0) = I_{6\times6}$$
Jacobian structure $$F = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{0}_{3\times3} & \mathbf{I}_{3\times3} \\ \frac{\partial \mathbf{a}}{\partial \mathbf{r}} & \mathbf{0}_{3\times3} \end{bmatrix}$$

The STM partitions into position-position (Φ_rr), position-velocity (Φ_rv), etc.

📊
Covariance Propagation

Uncertainty grows over time. The 6×6 covariance matrix evolves as:

Covariance evolution $$P(t) = \Phi(t, t_0) \cdot P(t_0) \cdot \Phi(t, t_0)^T + Q$$

Q is process noise modeling unmodeled forces. Position uncertainty typically grows from ~100m to ~1km over 24 hours.

Probability of Collision (Pc) — The Core Metric

The 2D Gaussian integral over the combined hard-body cross-section in the encounter plane:

Alfriend/Akella formulation $$P_c = \frac{1}{2\pi|C_{2D}|^{1/2}} \iint_A \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^T C_{2D}^{-1} \mathbf{x}\right) d\xi\, d\zeta$$

Where:

Combined covariance in encounter plane $$C_{2D} = P_{proj} \cdot (C_1^{pos} + C_2^{pos}) \cdot P_{proj}^T$$
Integration domain $$A = \{(\xi, \zeta) : \xi^2 + \zeta^2 \leq (R_1 + R_2)^2\}$$

Computed numerically via eigendecomposition + polar coordinate integration (50 radial × 100 angular samples).

📈
Composite Risk Score
Risk formulation $$\text{Risk}_j = P_{c,j} \times N_{\text{debris}} \times C_{\text{cascade}}$$
Cascade factor $$C_{\text{cascade}} = 1 + \rho_{\text{shell}} \cdot \log_{10}(\tau_{\text{lifetime}})$$

Debris in crowded, long-lived orbital shells receives exponentially higher risk weighting.

💣
NASA Breakup Fragment Count
Catastrophic collision $$N(L > L_c) = 0.1 \cdot M_{\text{total}}^{0.75} \cdot L_c^{-1.71}$$
Non-catastrophic collision $$N(L > L_c) = 0.1 \cdot m_p^{0.75} \cdot v_{\text{rel}}^{0.5} \cdot L_c^{-1.71}$$

Example: 310 kg catastrophic collision → ~360 trackable fragments (>10 cm).

🚀 Collision Avoidance Maneuver Planning

STM-based optimal maneuver direction, timing optimization, fuel-constrained multi-conjunction joint planning.

🎯
Maneuver Effectiveness (STM-based)

How a velocity change at maneuver time maps to position change at TCA:

Position sensitivity to Δv $$\Delta\mathbf{r}(t_{\text{TCA}}) = \Phi_{rv}(t_{\text{TCA}}, t_{\text{man}}) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{v}(t_{\text{man}})$$

Φ_rv is the upper-right 3×3 block of the State Transition Matrix. It tells us exactly how effective each burn direction is.

📌
Optimal Δv Direction

Maximize miss distance change per unit of fuel expended:

Optimal burn direction $$\hat{\Delta v}_{\text{opt}} = \frac{\Phi_{rv}^T \cdot \hat{m}}{|\Phi_{rv}^T \cdot \hat{m}|}$$

Where $\hat{m}$ is the unit miss vector direction. This pushes the encounter geometry apart most efficiently.

⏲
Maneuver Timing

Earlier maneuvers are more efficient — small Δv accumulates into large position change:

Rule of thumb $$\Delta_{\text{miss}} \approx |\Phi_{rv}| \cdot |\Delta v| \propto (t_{\text{TCA}} - t_{\text{man}})$$

1 cm/s burn, 1 orbit before TCA → ~100 m miss distance change. The code samples effectiveness across candidate times and picks the maximum.

🔗
Multi-Conjunction Joint Optimization

A single maneuver can resolve multiple conjunctions. Nelder-Mead optimization:

Objective function $$\min_{\Delta\mathbf{v}} \sum_j e^{-d_j / 0.5\text{km}} + 0.1\left(\frac{|\Delta\mathbf{v}|}{\Delta v_{\text{avail}}}\right)^2$$

Simultaneously minimizes all conjunction risks (exponential penalty for small miss distances) while penalizing fuel usage quadratically.

💥 Damage Minimization

When collision is unavoidable: NASA breakup model physics, impact geometry optimization, and five ranked mitigation strategies.

Specific Collision Energy
$$E_{\text{spec}} = \frac{\frac{1}{2} m_p v_{\text{rel}}^2}{m_t}$$
Energy per unit target mass
Catastrophic Threshold
$$E_{\text{cat}} = 40{,}000 \text{ J/kg}$$
Above this: complete fragmentation of both objects
Fragment Size Distribution
$$p(L) \propto L^{-2.71}$$
Power law — many small, few large fragments
Fragment Ejection Velocity
$$\sigma_v \approx 0.3\sqrt{\frac{E_{\text{spec}}}{E_{\text{cat}}}} \text{ km/s}$$
Log-normal distribution around parent velocity
🔄
1. Minimum Cross-Section (Free)

Rotate to present smallest face to impact direction. Zero fuel cost.

$$A_{\text{eff}} = A_{\text{min}} \approx \frac{A_{\text{nominal}}}{5}$$

Reduces collision probability by ~80% and damage by ~55%. Always the first action taken.

↗
2. Glancing Impact Geometry

Shift trajectory for tangential rather than head-on collision:

$$E_{\text{effective}} = E \cdot \sin^2(\theta)$$

At θ = 15° (glancing): energy reduction of 93%. Only ~0.5 m/s Δv needed for the geometry shift.

⏬
3. Reduce Relative Velocity

Burn anti-parallel to relative velocity to lower impact energy:

$$N_{\text{fragments}} \propto v_{\text{rel}}^{0.5}$$

Crossing the catastrophic threshold (40 kJ/kg) is the key goal. Below it: localized damage, not complete fragmentation.

⬇
4. Lower Collision Altitude

Move collision point to lower altitude where drag removes debris quickly:

AltitudeDebris Lifetime
< 300 kmDays to weeks
350-400 km~1 year
600-800 km25-200 years
> 900 km1000+ years
🔴
5. Controlled Deorbit (Last Resort)

Sacrifice the spacecraft to prevent the collision entirely:

Deorbit Δv (Hohmann) $$\Delta v = v_c\left(1 - \sqrt{\frac{r_{\text{target}}}{r_{\text{current}}}}\right)$$

Typically requires 100-200 m/s — often exceeds remaining budget. Eliminates collision but ends the mission.

💫
Debris Orbital Spread

Fragment ejection velocities change orbital elements:

$$\Delta a \approx \frac{2a^2 v}{\mu} \Delta v_T$$
$$\Delta i \approx \frac{r\cos(\omega+\nu)}{na^2\sqrt{1-e^2}} \Delta v_N$$

Tangential Δv spreads altitude; Normal Δv spreads inclination. Creates a debris "shell" around the collision point.

⚡
Kessler Syndrome Detection

Per altitude shell (50 km bands), check stability:

Population dynamics $$\frac{dN}{dt} = S(t) - D(t) + C(t)$$
Instability condition $$C(t) = k \cdot N^2 \cdot v_{\text{rel}} \cdot \sigma > D(t)$$

When collision-generated fragments exceed drag removal → runaway growth. Currently critical at 800-1000 km.

▶
Strategy 1: Greedy

Handle highest-risk conjunction first, iterate. Simple baseline.

for conj in sorted(conjunctions, key=risk):
  if should_maneuver(conj):
    maneuver = design_avoidance(conj)
    apply(maneuver) # Updates state

~90% optimal. O(N log N) complexity. Used for ≤5 conjunctions.

🔨
Strategy 2: Network Flow

Formulated as min-cost maximum flow:

  • • Source → conjunction nodes (capacity = risk)
  • • Conjunction → spacecraft nodes (who can resolve)
  • • Spacecraft → sink (capacity = fuel budget)

Solves via nx.maximum_flow(). Optimal fuel allocation across constellation. Used for 5-50 conjunctions.

🎲
Strategy 3: Monte Carlo Tree Search

Same algorithm that beat humans at Go. Explores intervention sequences:

UCB1 selection criterion $$\text{UCB1} = \frac{Q}{N} + c\sqrt{\frac{\ln N_{\text{parent}}}{N}}$$

100 simulations × max depth 10. Each simulation: select → expand → rollout → backpropagate. Handles sequential dependencies and cascade effects. Used for 50+ conjunctions.

⛽
Dynamic Fuel Allocation
$$\text{Reserve}_i = \underbrace{0.3 \cdot \Delta v_{\text{remaining}}}_{\text{base reserve}} + \underbrace{\min\left(N_{\text{expected}} \cdot 5\text{m/s},\; 0.5 \cdot \Delta v_{\text{remaining}}\right)}_{\text{risk-adjusted}}$$

Spacecraft in crowded shells or facing many future conjunctions maintain larger reserves. Prevents fuel exhaustion before mission end.
