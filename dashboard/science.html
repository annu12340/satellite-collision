Orbital mechanics explained like you're at a party and someone asked "so what do you actually do?"

🌍

Orbits: Falling With Style

STEP 1

A satellite isn't floating. It's not held up by anything. It's literally falling toward Earth every single second. But here's the trick: it's also moving sideways so fast that by the time it falls, the ground has curved away underneath it.

It's basically the world's most dramatic trust fall, except the Earth keeps dodging.

🎯 Real-World Analogy

Imagine throwing a ball so hard that by the time it should hit the ground, the Earth has curved away. Throw it at 28,000 km/h and congratulations — you just made an orbit. Also you have a very strong arm.

acceleration = -μ / r²   (always pointing at Earth's center)

That μ = 398,600 km³/s² is Earth's "gravitational pull strength." The bigger this number, the more Earth yanks on stuff. Jupiter's is 126 million. Jupiter doesn't mess around.

🤓 Nerd Moment

A satellite in Low Earth Orbit completes a full lap around Earth every ~90 minutes. That's 16 sunrises per day. Astronauts on the ISS see a sunrise every hour and a half. Their circadian rhythm is basically vibes at this point.

🌪️

Why Reality Ruins Perfect Circles

STEP 2

In a physics textbook, orbits are clean ellipses. In reality, three things conspire to make everything messier:

  1. Earth is chunky at the equator (J2) — Earth isn't a perfect sphere, it's a bit squished. That extra belly fat pulls on satellites unevenly, making orbital planes wobble like a drunk hula hoop.
  2. Air drag (yes, in space) — At 400 km altitude there are still a few sad, lonely air molecules. At 28,000 km/h, even those few molecules create drag. It's like driving through the world's thinnest car wash — barely noticeable, but over months it slows you down until you fall out of orbit.
  3. Sunlight physically pushes things — Photons have momentum. They literally push on satellites. Tiny force, but it adds up. The Sun is that coworker who pokes you gently but won't stop.
💡 Why This Matters

Because of these perturbations, we can't just calculate where a satellite will be next week with a simple formula. We have to simulate every tiny force, step by step, second by second. It's like predicting weather, but for individual metal boxes traveling at bullet-speed × 30.

🔮

The "Where IS It Though?" Problem

STEP 3

We never know exactly where a satellite is. Tracking stations measure it, but every measurement has errors. So instead of saying "the satellite is HERE," we say "the satellite is probably in this fuzzy cloud of possibilities."

That fuzzy cloud? It's called a covariance matrix. It's a 6×6 grid of numbers that says "position could be off by X in this direction, Y in that direction, and here's how those errors are related."

🎯 Real-World Analogy

You're tracking your friend via "Find My Friends" but the GPS dot is a big blue circle. That circle IS the covariance. And in space, the circle gets bigger the longer since the last GPS fix. Yesterday's location? Huge circle. Five minutes ago? Tiny circle.

The cruel part: uncertainty grows over time. A satellite we tracked 5 minutes ago? We know where it is within 10 meters. Same satellite, 24 hours later with no new data? Could be off by a full kilometer. The fuzzy cloud expands relentlessly.

💥

Finding Who's About to Crash

STEP 4

With 10,000 objects in orbit, there are ~50 million possible collision pairs. Checking all of them thoroughly would take forever. So we use a fast-food drive-through approach:

  1. Quick height check: If satellite A never goes above 500 km and satellite B never goes below 600 km, they physically can't meet. Bye. Next pair.
  2. Coarse time scan: For remaining pairs, check their distance every few minutes over the next 24 hours. Anyone that gets within 5 km gets flagged.
  3. Precision math: For flagged pairs, find the exact closest approach moment and calculate the actual probability of collision.
🎰 Probability of Collision

The "Pc" calculation is basically: take the two fuzzy clouds at closest approach, overlay them, and ask "what fraction of possibilities result in them overlapping?" If the answer is more than 1-in-10,000 (Pc > 10&sup-4;), it's time to panic productively.

🎯 Real-World Analogy

Two cars approaching an intersection, both with GPS errors. One might be in lane 1, 2, or 3. The other might arrive 2 seconds early or late. The "probability of collision" is the chance their error clouds overlap at the same spot at the same time. Except the cars are going 28,000 km/h and the intersection is in space.

🏎️

The Art of Space Dodging

STEP 5

When we fire a thruster, we change velocity by a tiny amount (called Δv, "delta-v"). This doesn't instantly move the satellite — instead, it slowly shifts the orbit so that hours later, the satellite is in a completely different spot.

1 cm/s burn now → ~100 meters of miss distance later

Timing is everything. An early nudge is worth way more than a last-second swerve. Same principle as steering a ship — adjust the wheel 1 degree now and you miss the iceberg by a mile. Wait until it's 10 meters away and you're on a Titanic sequel.

⛽ The Fuel Budget Problem

A typical small satellite has 25 m/s of Δv for its entire lifetime (years of operations). Each dodge costs 0.5-5 m/s. That's like having a 10-gallon gas tank that has to last 5 years, and every pothole requires a gallon to avoid. You become VERY picky about which potholes matter.

Risk Level Probability Action
CRITICAL Pc > 10&sup-4; (1 in 10,000) DODGE. Now. Burn fuel.
ELEVATED Pc 10&sup-5; to 10&sup-4; Maybe dodge? Depends on fuel budget.
ACCEPTABLE Pc < 10&sup-5; Accept it. Save fuel for scarier stuff.
🛡️

When You Can't Dodge: Damage Control

STEP 6

Sometimes there's no fuel left, or the warning comes too late. When a collision is unavoidable, the goal shifts from "avoid it" to "make it as gentle as possible."

At 10 km/s relative speed, a 10 kg object hitting a 300 kg satellite releases energy equivalent to 7 sticks of dynamite. Both objects shatter into thousands of pieces.

E = ½ × m × v²   →   1,670,000 J/kg (catastrophic threshold: 40,000 J/kg)

Five strategies, ranked from "free" to "painful":

  1. Turn sideways (💰 Free): Present your thinnest edge. Like turning a book edge-on to a thrown ball instead of catching it flat.
  2. Make it a glancing blow (💰 Cheap): A 15° glancing angle reduces effective impact energy by 93%. Car sideswipe vs. head-on.
  3. Slow down the approach (💰💰 Moderate): Reduce relative speed. Problem: you have a hand fan against a 10 km/s bullet. It helps, just... not a lot.
  4. Push collision lower (💰💰💰 Expensive): Make debris at low altitude where air drag cleans it up in months, not centuries.
  5. Sacrifice the spacecraft (💰💰💰💰 Nuclear option): Deorbit yourself before impact. No satellite = no collision. Mission over though.
🧠

The AI Decision Engine

STEP 7

The real challenge isn't handling ONE potential collision. It's handling hundreds simultaneously, with shared fuel budgets, interacting consequences, and cascading risks.

Dodging satellite A away from debris B might push A toward satellite C. Spending fuel now means less fuel for an unknown future threat. It's a chess game where every move changes the board.

🎲 Three Strategies We Use

Greedy: Handle the scariest thing first. Fast, simple, usually good enough. Like paying off your highest-interest credit card first.

Network Flow: Model the whole problem as a graph and solve it optimally. Like a GPS that sees ALL traffic and finds the globally fastest routes for everyone simultaneously.

Monte Carlo Tree Search: The same algorithm that beats humans at Go. Explore thousands of "what if I do X, then Y, then Z..." scenarios and pick the sequence with the best outcome.

☠️

Kessler Syndrome: The Nightmare

BONUS HORROR

In 1978, NASA scientist Donald Kessler proposed a terrifying scenario: what if collisions create so much debris that the debris causes MORE collisions, which create MORE debris, in a runaway chain reaction?

Eventually, certain orbital altitudes become permanent minefields that no spacecraft can safely use. For centuries. Maybe longer.

🎯 Real-World Analogy

Imagine a highway where every car crash creates 1,000 new invisible bumper cars that ricochet randomly at bullet speed. Eventually the highway becomes permanently unusable, and the ricocheting bumper cars start hitting cars on adjacent highways too. That's Kessler Syndrome.

The scariest part? The altitude band of 800-1000 km is already dangerously close to this tipping point. This system's job is to keep us on the safe side of that line.

📊 Real Numbers

One collision between two 1-ton satellites at orbital speed produces ~360 fragments larger than 10 cm (trackable) and ~18,000 fragments larger than 1 cm (lethal but invisible). Each one is a potential trigger for the next collision.

🚀 TL;DR

Thousands of objects zooming at 28,000 km/h. Can't easily stop. Limited fuel. Uncertain positions. Every crash makes it worse. Our job: figure out who's about to hit who, pick the smartest dodges, and keep the cascading space junkyard scenario from becoming reality.

Fuel is finite. Threats are infinite. Every decision has consequences for the future.
That's what makes it an AI problem.
