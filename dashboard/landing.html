The Problem

The world's worst highway

No lanes. No brakes. No rearview mirrors. Just thousands of spacecraft doing 28,000 km/h, running on a fuel tank the size of an energy drink — one that has to last years. Clip another car up here and you don't crumple a fender. You detonate into a few thousand bullets and hand them to everyone else on the road.

01
Chapter 1 — orbital_mechanics.py

Falling, with style

Every satellite is just falling — sideways, fast enough to keep missing the ground forever. We simulate that fall precisely: Earth's equatorial bulge, atmospheric drag, solar pressure, and the fuzziness of "where exactly is this thing" growing over time.

02
Chapter 2 — conjunction.py

Needle, meet haystack

50 million possible pairs. Cheap geometric filters knock out almost all of them instantly. What survives gets the real math — the actual probability-of-collision integral, the same one real operators trust. Cross 1-in-10,000 odds, and the system raises an eyebrow.

03
Chapter 3 — avoidance.py

The tiny nudge that saves the day

You can't juke sideways in orbit. You nudge your velocity a whisper, early, and let the universe turn it into a big miss. Most satellites get 25 m/s of fuel for their entire life — a shot glass, not a gas tank. So we only spend it when the math says it's worth the sip.

04
Chapter 4 — damage_minimization.py

Plan B, when Plan A fails

Sometimes you can't dodge. The system runs NASA's real breakup model to make the crash suck less: turn edge-on, aim for a glancing sideswipe, or bias the wreck toward an altitude where drag cleans up in months instead of centuries.

05
Chapter 5 — risk_optimizer.py

The chess master

Hundreds of collisions at once, all sharing the same fuel, all tangled together. We treat the constellation like a risk network, find the clusters, and find the single maneuver that quietly defuses them all — using the same algorithm that beat humans at Go.

Chapter 6 — the dashboard

Show, don't just tell

Live 3D orbit views, risk timelines, and debris breakdowns — with an LLM narrator that turns raw numbers into something an operator can read on a bad day, without a physics degree.
